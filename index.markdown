# UBC Botanical Garden Plant Records Management Manual 
<center><img src="./figures/campbell.jpeg" alt="the campbell bldg" width="800"></center>

Plant records are as essential to a botanical garden as the living collections themselves. They document the identity, origin, history, and management of each accession, preserving the scientific, conservation, horticultural, and educational value of the collection over time. Plant documentation comprises the policies, databases, maps, images, and other associated records that support the management of living collections (Michener, 2011). Accurate, complete, and consistently maintained records enable effective collection management, support research and conservation initiatives, and facilitate the sharing of reliable information with staff, researchers, partner institutions, and the public (Highland, 2014). 

As living museums, botanical gardens have both an ethical and professional responsibility to maintain plant records that meet accepted standards. Without comprehensive documentation, living collections lose much of their scientific, historical, and interpretive value, limiting their usefulness for research, conservation, education, and public engagement (American Association of Museums, 1984, as cited in Hohn, 2008). This manual outlines the standards, procedures, and best practices for creating, maintaining, and managing plant records to ensure the long-term integrity, accessibility, and value of the living collections at the University of British Columbia Botanical Garden. 

## Plant Records History 

The University of British Columbia Botanical Garden has maintained plant accession records since the Garden's early years using a variety of documentation systems. The earliest accession records are preserved in five handwritten accession books, which document accessions 1964-0001 through 1995-0477. These volumes record basic accession information, including the taxon, accession date, and source, and are housed in the Accession Technician Office (Room 216). These books remain an important historical record of the Garden's living collections. 

In 1969, the Garden adopted the Botanical Garden Accession System (BGAS), a custom in-house database developed specifically to manage plant records. Over time, three versions of BGAS were developed, with BGAS3 serving as the primary collections management system until 2015. Historical printouts from the 70’s archived in the Accession Technician Office, and digital backups of the BGAS3 database from 2014 have been retained. The original BGAS3 software may still be operable on a legacy laptop maintained by the Garden. 

In 2010, the Garden also evaluated Bauble, an open-source collections management program originally developed for the Belize Botanic Garden. Although Bauble offered functionality that was not available in BGAS, it was used on an experimental basis while BGAS remained the official record system. Following evaluation, Bauble was determined to be insufficient for the Garden's collections management requirements and was not adopted as the primary database. 

Between 2014 and 2015, the Garden transitioned to IrisBG, the current collections management system. This migration included the transfer of accession data from BGAS3 and the adoption of a new accession numbering convention. Under BGAS, accession numbers consisted of the accession year, a five-digit sequential accession number, and a source number. IrisBG introduced a new format in which accession numbers consist of the accession year followed by a four-digit sequential number that restarts at 0001 each calendar year. Historical BGAS accession numbers are retained within IrisBG as the conserved accession number and remain searchable. 

Following the migration in 2015, the Accession Technician, Eric Lafountain, reviewed and corrected data transfer issues. However, users should be aware that records for accessions created prior to May 2015 may contain historical inconsistencies resulting from ambiguities in the original BGAS data or limitations encountered during the migration process. Consequently, accession histories for older records should be interpreted with appropriate caution and, where necessary, verified against original documentation.
